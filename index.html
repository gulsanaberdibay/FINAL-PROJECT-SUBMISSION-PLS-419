01 / THE PROBLEM

The air is shared.
The protection is not.

Almaty’s winter smog is an environmental problem with a social structure. This project focuses on outdoor fine particulate matter, PM₂.₅, during the heating season in Almaty and its urban fringe. It asks how public institutions can reduce exposure among schoolchildren without transferring responsibility to families who cannot afford private protection. The proposed intervention is a school pilot, rather than a claim to solve every source of urban pollution.

PM₂.₅ describes particles with diameters of approximately 2.5 micrometres or smaller. WHO’s 2021 guidelines recommend an annual mean of 5 µg/m³ and a 24-hour level of 15 µg/m³. These are different averaging periods, not interchangeable warning thresholds. Fine-particle exposure matters for cardiovascular and respiratory health; meeting a guideline does not establish that every individual faces zero risk. [1]

Evidence from three complementary sources establishes the issue. The World Bank identifies coal combustion in power and household heating as major contributors to Almaty’s particulate exposure. Kerimray and colleagues document pronounced seasonal and spatial differences. Tursumbayeva and colleagues link winter pollution with weak atmospheric ventilation. Emissions and dispersion interact: mountains influence how pollution disperses, but do not generate the emissions. [2][3][4]

The scale is citywide, but exposure and protection vary by location and activity. Residents, commuters, outdoor workers, and pupils do not experience identical conditions. The headline figure on this page—94.0 µg/m³—is a historical winter mean from one study, not a current citywide reading. It establishes severity within that study’s record. It must not be converted into an unsupported count of deaths or illnesses.

The clearest temporal pattern in the reviewed evidence is seasonal deterioration in winter. A continuous worsening trend through 2026 cannot be inferred from studies using different years and instruments. Changes in wind, heating demand, measurement coverage, and equipment can alter reported averages. Explaining those differences is more defensible than presenting a dramatic but inconsistent time series.

The social difficulty is unequal capacity to respond. An alert assumes someone can change their activities or access cleaner indoor air. Families may lack affordable heating alternatives, and schools may lack maintained filtration. These are mechanisms the project proposes to investigate, not measured claims about every household. Treating protection as an individual purchase risks rewarding existing advantage.

This scope also establishes the limits of the claim. A school forecast cannot replace affordable clean heating, industrial emission controls, or reliable public transport. Those measures address production of pollution; the pilot addresses an institution’s response to exposure. The proposal should compete honestly for public funds against those alternatives, not use technological novelty to avoid discussing priorities. Its justification depends on additional, measurable protection.

THE SOCIAL-SCIENCE QUESTION

Under what institutional and funding conditions can air-quality forecasts reduce unequal exposure in Almaty’s public schools, rather than simply shifting responsibility onto families and teachers?

Schools provide a practical setting because one public institution can coordinate protection for many children. The relevant political questions concern budget allocation, institutional duties, and whose needs determine priorities. A forecast becomes a public benefit only when a responsible organisation has resources to act. This distinction connects atmospheric evidence to the project’s central concern: accountable collective protection.

Original Figure 1 compares published seasonal means from Tursumbayeva et al. The winter value is 94.0 µg/m³ and the summer value is 9.9 µg/m³: a ratio of approximately 9.49. The chart deliberately uses a zero baseline and reproduces the underlying values below. It is a new visualisation of real published data, not a new measurement campaign. [4]

The contrast explains why the intervention prioritises the heating season. It does not reveal what fraction of the difference comes from fuel combustion, because meteorology changes simultaneously. Comparing a seasonal mean directly with an annual guideline would also confuse averaging periods. The figure supports targeting an operational problem while preserving uncertainty about its precise causal composition.

For the pilot, every record would include time, location, instrument identity, units, and a quality flag. An agreed completeness rule would determine which days enter comparisons. Changes to placement or correction methods would be logged rather than hidden inside a revised average. Public exports would include a data dictionary and version history. This protocol is a proposed safeguard, not a description of all existing datasets. Its purpose is to make an independent analyst able to reconstruct the published results and identify where missing information changes the conclusion.

Four silences in the evidence

Spatial coverage. A monitor observes its immediate environment. An average across available stations can overrepresent well-monitored neighbourhoods. Urban-fringe homes, school entrances, and outdoor workplaces may remain insufficiently characterised. Interpolation fills a map, not an evidentiary gap. The proposed service would distinguish observations from predictions and mark locations without adequate validation.

Measurement quality. Lower-cost instruments require comparison with reference equipment. Humidity, ageing, placement, and maintenance can influence performance. Missing observations must remain missing, rather than silently becoming clean-air periods. Publishing raw readings, correction methods, uptime, and calibration histories would allow others to evaluate the evidence rather than merely trust a coloured display.

Source attribution. Tonnes emitted are different from micrograms per cubic metre inhaled. Stack height, atmospheric chemistry, transport, and population location affect exposure. National stationary-source totals exclude important dimensions of local experience. A high concentration at a school cannot identify a particular household or enterprise as responsible without additional evidence.

Social and indoor conditions. Outdoor monitors do not reveal classroom ventilation, filtration, household resources, or whether staff can follow advice. The pilot would add indoor monitoring in participating rooms and voluntary interviews about barriers to action. These would investigate response capacity, not diagnose individual children or infer household poverty from their addresses.

Trust should therefore be graded by purpose. Official statistics are useful for institutional reporting; research campaigns clarify mechanisms; local reference comparisons support operational decisions. No single dataset establishes all three. A credible evaluation needs stable measurement procedures and matched periods, with weather and equipment changes documented before interpreting an apparent improvement as policy success.

03 / CURRENT STATE OF RESEARCH

Prediction is not explanation.
Explanation is not protection.

The research review combines Kazakhstan-specific evidence, a regional comparison with Astana, and international methodological studies. Six academic studies are organised by approach rather than presented as isolated summaries. Their disagreements concern what a method can establish: description, attribution, prediction, or evaluation. Distinguishing these purposes prevents a technically impressive model from carrying an unsupported policy claim.

Patterns and atmospheric mechanisms

Kerimray et al. (2020a) examine multiple pollutants across space and time in Almaty. Their observed seasonal patterns and elevation relationships suggest that one city average conceals meaningful variation. However, associations between elevation, season, and concentration cannot independently establish exact source shares. The study is strongest for describing patterns and identifying hypotheses for further investigation. [3]

Tursumbayeva et al. (2022) examine the planetary boundary layer, the lower atmospheric region in which mixing occurs. Their observations associate high winter pollution with restricted ventilation. This supports including meteorological predictors in a forecast. It does not establish a permanent prediction rule: a limited observation period cannot represent every unusual winter or future change in emissions. [4]

Together, these studies imply that the proposal needs physical context as well as statistical fitting. A model might learn calendar patterns that fail when weather or heating practices change. Equally, describing natural ventilation constraints does not absolve institutions from controlling emissions. Atmospheric vulnerability and political responsibility operate at different explanatory levels.

Source identification and interrupted activity

Kerimray et al. (2020b) examine Almaty during COVID restrictions. Reduced mobility did not eliminate particulate pollution, challenging a traffic-only explanation. Nevertheless, lockdown was not a randomised transport experiment: weather and other activities changed too. The study supports a multi-source interpretation, not the conclusion that transport is irrelevant or that one observed percentage represents traffic’s permanent contribution. [7]

Tursun et al. (2025) analyse particle chemistry in Almaty and Astana using Positive Matrix Factorization and air-mass trajectories. Their sampling identifies multiple contributing factors and highlights combustion. The regional comparison helps distinguish shared energy-system concerns from city-specific atmospheric conditions. Statistical factors still require interpretation, and sampled conditions cannot automatically represent every neighbourhood throughout the year. [8]

The two approaches are complementary. Interrupted activity asks what changes under unusual conditions; chemical analysis investigates mixtures present in collected particles. Neither turns a concentration forecast into evidence against a particular polluter. That distinction constrains the proposal: predictions guide protective responses, while enforcement requires separate inspection and legally sufficient attribution.

Calibration and credible evaluation

Zimmerman et al. (2018) test random-forest calibration for lower-cost multipollutant gas instruments. Their findings support combining machine learning with controlled measurement procedures. They do not directly validate the PM₂.₅ devices proposed here. Transferring the method requires local comparisons, suitable reference equipment, and evaluation under Almaty’s operating conditions rather than borrowing another instrument’s reported accuracy. [9]

Grange and Carslaw (2019) use meteorological normalisation to examine air-quality interventions in Britain. Accounting for weather reveals changes that raw time series can obscure. This is valuable when a windy winter might otherwise resemble policy success. Nevertheless, statistical adjustment depends on relevant predictors and assumptions; it does not eliminate unmeasured changes or automatically establish causation. [10]

These international methods change the evaluation design. Calibration must precede claims about neighbourhood differences. Assessment must compare forecasts with simple alternatives and separate prediction performance from intervention outcomes. A small average error can coexist with missed severe episodes; consequently, aggregate accuracy alone is an inadequate measure of public value.

A further distinction concerns what counts as success. Forecast research can optimise an error statistic while overlooking whether warnings arrive before school decisions. Source research can identify combustion without explaining whether households can switch fuels. An institutional evaluation must connect those findings to feasible action. The proposal therefore treats technical accuracy as necessary evidence, while asking separately about distribution, workload, and implementation. This synthesis is an analytical choice: it follows from the different questions these methods answer, rather than from a single study claiming to resolve them all.

What remains unresolved

The reviewed evidence supports a seasonally intensified, multi-source problem shaped by emissions and ventilation. Exact source percentages remain sensitive to method, location, and period. The studies should not be forced into one ranking. A comparable uncertainty surrounds intervention effects: better monitoring can document a problem without changing anyone’s ability to avoid it.

The project addresses the connection between information and institutional action. The six studies do not establish whether a forecast linked to funded school responses would distribute protection fairly in Almaty. This is a gap in the reviewed evidence, not a claim that no relevant research exists. The contribution would be a locally evaluated governance arrangement, rather than another algorithm claiming universal accuracy.

04 / THE AI-ASSISTED SOLUTION & ITS COST

Almaty Air Commons.
A forecast with a duty to act.

The system and its public purpose

Fund a twelve-month pilot in twenty public schools, supported by sixty supplementary outdoor sensors, classroom measurements, and access to reference and meteorological data. These are proposed parameters, not an existing programme. Select schools using published exposure and protection-need criteria, with geographic coverage and community input. Participation should not depend on parents purchasing equipment or owning smartphones.

A compact machine-learning model would predict next-day PM₂.₅ using recent concentrations, forecast weather, calendar variables, and fixed geographic characteristics. It would produce uncertainty intervals and data-quality flags. A persistence forecast, assuming tomorrow resembles today, would remain the baseline. Training and evaluation periods would be separated chronologically to prevent future information leaking into apparent performance.

The municipality would own the service; a university team would validate it; contractors would maintain instruments and software. Health specialists would approve bilingual response guidance. School staff would decide how to adjust outdoor activities and operate available filtration. Missing or unreliable data would trigger a clearly labelled fallback, never fabricated readings. This website presents the proposal and evidence, not live forecasts.

Two documented implementations abroad

BEIJING / CHINA

IBM Green Horizons

IBM and Beijing’s environmental authority implemented machine-learning-supported forecasting and decision support. IBM’s 2015 account documents one-kilometre forecasts seventy-two hours ahead and an alert workflow. The achieved capability was operational forecasting. The same announcement reports a city pollution reduction, but does not isolate AI’s contribution. Transfer the workflow, not a promised emissions effect. [11]

SOUTH KOREA / OPERATIONAL FORECASTING

AI alongside atmospheric models

A 2026 operational-system evaluation compares numerical forecasts with LSTM and Transformer models. It reports an LSTM F1-score of 79% in the Seoul metropolitan area and improved severe-event detection from the Transformer. These are forecasting outcomes, not reductions in disease. The transferable lesson is to evaluate different error types instead of presenting one accuracy number as universal success. [12]

Both cases establish implementation in a similar sphere. Neither proves that importing a model improves Almaty’s schools. Institutional capacity, instrument quality, and local validation remain necessary. The pilot would publish forecast error, missed episodes, false alarms, and results by location. Staged introduction of supported responses would permit comparisons while preserving existing essential guidance for every school.

The evaluation would measure indoor concentrations, response completion, staff workload, and access to protection. A proposed technical target is ten percent lower mean absolute error than persistence on held-out winter data, alongside an acceptable missed-alert rate agreed with health specialists. This is a design target. No mortality reduction, achieved accuracy, or causal exposure benefit is claimed in advance.

THE PUBLIC BILL12-month pilot · all proposal prices are assumptions

Implementation would begin with permissions, co-design, reference comparisons, and a published analysis plan. The next phase would run forecasts silently so errors can be studied before staff rely on them. Supported alerts would then begin in stages, followed by a public evaluation. Procurement would require incident logs, transferable documentation, and access for independent reviewers. These stages are proposed safeguards rather than fixed promises about data availability. If reference access or adequate calibration cannot be secured, deployment should pause instead of presenting unreliable estimates as precise local knowledge.

Environmental: count the whole service

At three watts continuously, sixty sensors consume 1,576.8 kWh annually. A 150-watt server with a facility overhead multiplier of 1.4 consumes 1,839.6 kWh; add 100 kWh for training. Digital operations total 3,516.4 kWh. These are transparent engineering assumptions, not equipment measurements. Repeated retraining would require additional metering and justification.

KEGOC reports 74.4% thermal generation and 11.0% gas-turbine generation in its 2025 breakdown. The thermal category is not an exact coal share. Electricity consumption therefore cannot be treated as carbon-free. An illustrative factor of 0.8 kg CO₂e/kWh gives 2.81 tonnes for digital operations; this is sensitivity analysis, not an official emissions inventory. [13]

Twenty assumed forty-watt filters, operated eight hours daily for 180 days, add 1,152 kWh. Combined operational use becomes 4,668.4 kWh, implying 3.73 tonnes under the same hypothetical factor. Actual filtration needs depend on room volume and device performance. These figures exclude embodied emissions, replacement filters, transport, and equipment disposal.

Water cannot honestly be assigned a single measured value before hosting is chosen. At an illustrative zero-to-two litres per server-facility kWh, direct server cooling implies zero to 3.68 m³ annually. This excludes electricity-generation and manufacturing water. Procurement should require location-specific energy and cooling disclosures, repairability, and documented disposal rather than advertising an unsupported environmental benefit.

The municipality would fund the pilot, potentially with research support. Contractors profit through published service fees, not advertising or selling personal data. An open tender, exportable records, documented code, and an exit clause would reduce dependence on one supplier. Before renewal, compare recurring costs and demonstrated benefits with simpler monitoring and direct protective spending.

Labor: automation changes responsibility

Technicians install, clean, calibrate, and replace instruments. Analysts investigate anomalies; developers maintain pipelines; translators and health professionals review messages. School staff need paid time, training, and backup cover. Fault labelling, complaint handling, and evaluation are hidden labor unless contracts name them. The proposal does not assume job losses; it redistributes tasks and must avoid adding unpaid duties to already busy employees.

Political governance: apply the Week 12 frameworks

Buribayeva, Khamzina and Buribayev identify institutional opacity, fragmented responsibility, and infrastructural dependence as Kazakhstan-specific risks. Their analysis anticipates vulnerabilities rather than measuring harm from this proposal. The response here is a published responsibility register: name who approves alerts, investigates failures, authorises changes, and can stop operation. An independent reviewer must be able to challenge the contractor. [14]

Roberts et al. favour coordination and capacity-building among existing international institutions. Applied by analogy locally, the pilot should strengthen cooperation among monitoring bodies, schools, municipal health authorities, and universities. Mhlambi’s relational perspective adds community well-being: parents, teachers, and maintenance workers should help define protection, rather than become passive recipients of automated instructions. These local applications are the project’s own interpretations. [15][16]

OECD Principles 1.1–1.5 translate into equitable school selection, privacy, understandable forecasts, secure operation, and identifiable accountability. UNESCO’s proportionality principle supports testing simpler alternatives; its human-oversight principle preserves public responsibility. Together they justify independent evaluation and a right to report problems. Neither framework makes the project beneficial simply because its stated purpose is environmental. [17][18]

Kazakhstan’s AI law supplies the domestic framework for transparency, risk management, and responsibility. Its exact application requires assessment of the deployed design. The EU AI Act supplies a comparison, not automatic jurisdiction: Articles 3, 5, and 6 and Annex III distinguish definitions, prohibited practices, and particular high-risk uses. A forecast is not automatically high-risk merely because a school receives it. [19][20]

The governance board should publish meeting decisions and report whose schools receive equipment first. Community consultation must influence actual choices, including alert timing and acceptable disruption, rather than merely endorse a completed design. Success indicators should include whether schools with fewer initial resources can act on the advice. An institution should not receive a poor performance label simply because its building requires investment the pilot cannot provide.

Data ethics: measure air, not children

The core model needs environmental observations, not names, diagnoses, faces, or movement histories. Obtain institutional permission for installation and informed consent for optional research participation, with appropriate parental consent and child assent where applicable. Public information should require no account. Separate contact details from survey responses, restrict access, and define deletion dates before collection.

Kazakhstan’s personal-data law requires a domestic database for personal-data storage. Local hosting alone does not ensure security or meaningful consent. Publish environmental data where safe, aggregate sensitive research findings, and prohibit household profiling or automated sanctions. A complaint route must work offline as well as online so access does not depend on a smartphone. [21]

THE VERDICT

Yes—only with funded protection.

Approve a limited pilot only if schools receive practical resources, staff are paid, independent evaluation is funded, privacy is protected, and contracts preserve public control. Stop or simplify the system if it adds little beyond baseline forecasts. Cleaner heating and source controls must continue independently. If these conditions cannot be met, direct spending on protection and emissions reduction is more defensible. The objective is lower, more fairly distributed exposure—not a more impressive dashboard.
